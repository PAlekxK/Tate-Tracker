## a · Recommendation — the greeting stays second-person and **flexes its number**

**`my home` and `your home(s)` are the same place from two seats.** The apex is the name *she says*
(*"open my home dot place"*); the greeting is what the product *says back to her*. They can never
contradict, because they never share a grammatical person — the collision only looks real if you read
the apex as a sentence addressed to her, and it isn't; §3b already ruled the landing surface carries a
greeting and not a brand. What does need fixing is the count: `your homes` is plural-only, §3b flagged
it, and a plural over one card is the on-vs-empty failure shape in copy. So: keep the word, flex the
number. One conditional, one lie removed.

| slot | ✅ recommended | alternate |
|---|---|---|
| **apex** `myhome.place` | *A private record of the places a family looks after — built by Paul Kirschenbauer, a household at a time. Nothing to sign up for; the doors here are opened by hand.* (+ *Reach Paul ›*) | same line, closing *"…opened by invitation."* |
| **family door**, one estate | **Your home** — over the single card | *(greeting suppressed; the card stands alone)* |
| **family door**, two estates | **Your homes** — over Fernwood and the condo | **Your homes** (fixed plural, never flexes) |

**The alternate is a real option, not a straw man:** one string, no conditional, and the one-estate
problem handled by *absence* — which is already this product's rule (the selector is absent at one
grant). It costs a blank-topped door for anyone who lands on the family URL directly. I'd still flex:
the singular is four characters of work and it is the honest string.

⚠️ **Scope note, so the one-estate row isn't over-read:** at one grant Mom lands in Fernwood and never
meets this surface at all. Her icon label stays **Fernwood Tracker** — her own word, untouched. The
singular greeting is a fallback for a one-estate family arriving at their door directly, not a screen
we are putting in front of her.

## b · Falsifier — inside a month, what says the pairing reads wrong

1. **Someone asks how to sign in, sign up, or get an account** after meeting the apex. That is `my`
   having been read as a portal, and it is the only failure that costs anything.
2. **She says the possessive back with the wrong owner** — *"is that your home or mine?"*, or she calls
   the app *My Home* instead of Fernwood. Either means the shell started competing with the room
   (§4a's claim failing), or the two seats collapsed into one.
3. **The two-estate door produces a question instead of a tap.** She reads *Your homes*, opens neither,
   and asks Paul which one to open. The jump strip is 5-for-5 and every ask-shaped affordance is 0 —
   a greeting that generates a question has become an ask.

*(Not a falsifier: her never mentioning the apex. She is not supposed to meet it.)*

## c · On `my` — how it stays a welcome and never an account

The research bar is right and it is narrower than it looks: what reads as an account is not the
possessive, it is **a possessive attached to a system noun** — *my account, my settings, my profile, my
dashboard*. That construction names the reader as the operator of her own life, which is §4's durable
reason for killing *estate manager* and its synonyms in one stroke. `my home` attaches to a **place**,
and a place is the one thing here she genuinely owns; that is why Paul's read holds and the bar still
stands. The discipline that keeps it true is a count, not a taste: **`my` appears exactly once in the
whole system — in the address — and nowhere in the shell's chrome.** A second `my` turns a name into a
pattern, and the pattern it becomes is the portal. So: the door speaks **your**, the address speaks
**my**, nothing behind either is labelled with a possessive, and any settings surface is named for the
thing it sets. If a future screen wants *My anything*, that is the tripwire, not a style call.
