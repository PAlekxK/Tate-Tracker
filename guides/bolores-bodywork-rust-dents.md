## Where this truck rusts (so the walk-around isn't blind)

OBS Broncos/F-series **rust from the inside out**, mostly at the **rear wheel arches** — the inner rear fenders are two overlapped pieces that trap mud/debris the tire throws up, and the top of the arch collects dirt that holds moisture. Also check: **lower front fenders behind the tires**, **cab corners**, **rockers**, **floor pans**, and **inside the rear quarters**. Surface bubbling on the outside usually means more is happening behind it.

*Prevention tip worth doing once it's clean:* drill three 1" holes along the top of the inner wheel house (where it meets the quarter skin), so you can hose/clean it out, and plug them with 1" rubber plugs. Keeps the arch from re-rotting.

### Understanding the bubbles (what you're actually looking at)

This is the single most important thing to internalize before the walk-around, because it changes how you *budget* the job:

- **A paint bubble is the tip, not the extent.** A bubble forms because rust under the paint has lost its bond and is expanding — rust takes up **far more volume than the steel it replaces**, so it physically pushes the paint up into a blister. The metal failure is already well underway by the time you see the surface move.
- **Rust spreads sideways under paint that still looks fine.** It creeps laterally along the panel beneath "healthy" paint. A coin-sized bubble routinely hides a much larger thin/perforated area — the classic "poke it and your finger goes through into a hole."
- **You cannot judge severity from the outside.** The only honest assessment is to **open it up** — sand/grind the bubble and the seemingly-good paint around it back to metal and keep going until you're into bright, solid steel on all sides. The true edge of the rust is always past where the paint looked bad.
- **Bubbles mean the back side is under attack too.** On these arches the rot comes from *inside* the two-piece fender, so an outside bubble is a signal to inspect the inner wheel house, not just the skin.

**Practical takeaway for scoping:** treat every wheel-arch bubble as "unknown until opened — assume bigger than it looks." Don't price or schedule the paint off the *visible* rust; price it off what you find once you've ground one or two bubbles back to clean metal. That's what tells us whether each arch is a Rung 1 (cleanup) or a Rung 2/3 (patch/weld) job.

## RUST — the DIY ladder

Work down this ladder. The rung you're on tells you whether it's ours or a hand-off.

**Rung 1 — Surface / scale rust, NO holes** → **fully DIY.**
1. Wire-wheel / sand / grind to **bright bare metal** (wherever there's any orange, keep going).
2. **Convert + neutralize** what's left in the pores: phosphoric-acid product (Ospho / "Prep & Etch"); let it cure, rinse/neutralize per label, dry fully.
3. **Epoxy primer** over the bare metal (direct-to-metal). Done — it goes to the booth from there.

**Rung 2 — Small perforation (a hole or two), non-structural panel** → **still DIY, two routes:**
- **(a) Bonded patch panel — no welding.** Cut back to solid metal, make a patch from like-gauge sheet, scuff/clean both mating surfaces, bond with **3M panel-bonding adhesive** + a few rivets/clecos to clamp while it cures. Modern panel adhesive tests **as strong as a weld with no warping**, and it seals the seam against moisture. **Rustproof the backside** of the repair (cavity wax / Fluid Film) so it doesn't rot from behind.
  - *Trade-off (from the forums):* panel bond is **less forgiving** than welds — you can't drill it back out if fitment's off, and it has a ~20-min working window. So **dry-fit and mark everything first**, then commit.
- **(b) Weld-in patch.** Stronger-feeling and the "right" classic fix, but needs a MIG welder + the skill to not blow through thin metal → for us that's a **hand-off** unless we decide to learn it on a scrap panel first.

**Rung 3 — Large perforated areas / the arch is mostly gone / structural rust** → **HAND OFF.** Full wheel-arch or patch-panel replacement with extensive welding and metal shaping. Rust through anything **structural** (frame, body mounts, suspension pickups, floor supports) is a safety item — that gets welded by someone who knows, no debate.

> **The rust hand-off line, in one sentence:** if it's *solid metal needing cleanup* (Rung 1) or *a small hole we can bond a patch into* (Rung 2a), it's ours; if it needs *real welding or metal fabrication* (Rung 2b/3) or it's *structural*, it's a hand-off.

## DENTS — easier than they look, because we're repainting

Since the panel's getting sprayed anyway, most small dents are just prep work. The one number that matters:

> **Body filler is only for dents ≤ 1/8" deep** (and never thicker than ~1/4" or it shrinks/cracks/pops out). Deeper than that → bring the metal *up* first, then fill.

**Rung 1 — Shallow dent (≤ 1/8"), DIY:**
1. Sand the area to **bare metal** (filler doesn't stick to paint).
2. Skim **body filler** into the low spot, build slightly proud.
3. Block-sand 80 → 180 → 320 until dead flat to the surrounding panel.
4. **Direct-to-metal primer** over any bare metal, scuff 220–300. Ready for the booth.

**Rung 2 — Deeper dent, DIY with patience:** knock/pull the metal back up to ≤ 1/8" *first* — hammer & dolly from behind, or a **dent puller** (slide-hammer or glue-pull) where you can't reach the back — then fill per Rung 1. Get it as close as possible before any filler goes on.

**Rung 3 — HAND OFF:** sharp **creases**, dents on a **body/character line** or swage, or **stretched metal** that won't return (oil-cans / pops). Those need shrinking/metal-finishing skill. If hammer-and-dolly isn't getting it close to flat, hand it over rather than burying a bad dent under a slab of filler.

## The bed floor by the tailgate (known spot — Paul flagged 2026-06-22)

There's a **small but significant rusty spot in the bed near the tailgate, where water got in.** This is a *textbook* full-size Bronco failure, not a mystery: the tailgate leaks — water runs between the glass and the outer sweep, through the **tailgate access panel** (its paper/vapor barrier fails), and drips down into the **rear bed corners**, which then rot from sitting wet. So we're almost certainly looking at the known tailgate-leak-rots-the-bed-corner pattern.

**Two jobs, and the order matters:**
1. **Fix the source first, or it rots again.** Before (or alongside) patching the metal, address the leak: inspect/replace the **tailgate weatherstrip — especially the corner pieces**, and redo the **access-panel barrier** (owners replace the failed paper barrier with plastic sheet sealed down with good tape). New metal over an unfixed leak just rusts back out.
2. **Repair the rusted metal.** Per the ladder above — open it up to see the true extent (remember: bigger than it looks). Best case it's surface scale (Rung 1, clean + convert + prime). If it's perforated (likely, since water pooled there), it's **Rung 2**: cut back to solid steel and either **bond a patch (3M panel adhesive, no welding)** or **weld a patch in** if we decide to. The bed floor isn't frame-structural, so a properly bonded-and-rustproofed patch is legitimate here.

**Status:** teed up — **Paul to provide photos + more detail** (size, whether it's gone through, how far the wet spread under surrounding paint/coating). That detail decides Rung 2-bond vs. weld vs. hand-off. Likely ours to handle with a cut-and-patch; we'll confirm once we can see it. Log it on the walk-around sheet with a Rung number once opened.
