## Property Card Lead (1 paragraph)

*The house sits at 2,959 feet on a south-facing shoulder of the Blue Ridge in Pickens County — land that was Cherokee Nation until 1838 and that became Tate Mountain Estates a century later, when Col. Sam Tate raised Lake Sequoyah a quarter-mile down the hill. The soil is Hayesville and Cecil and Pacolet — the red-clay subsoil and mica-flecked loam that this part of the Appalachians has been weathering out of gneiss and schist for a long time. Rain that falls on the fairway drains east into the headwaters of the Etowah; on clear nights the sky reads Bortle 3, one tier shy of the darkest in Georgia. It is a small piece of a large place, and the almanac that follows is an attempt to keep track of it.*

**Notes on the lead:**
- Italic Crimson Text recommended. Reads as the almanac's opening page rather than a card header.
- Four sentences. Opens with elevation + aspect + county (anchors *this* place), moves through history, then soil, then water and sky, lands on the almanac's purpose. Builds outward from the house.
- Final clause ("a small piece of a large place, and the almanac that follows is an attempt to keep track of it") is the Leopold register — quiet purpose, no marketing energy, names the form.
- Could-be-anyone test: passes. Address, elevation, soil series, Tate Mountain Estates, Etowah, Bortle 3 — none of this is portable to another property.

## Surface-Fact Callouts (6 subsections)

### 1. On Cherokee land
**Heading:** *On Cherokee land*
**Body:** This ground was Cherokee Nation territory from 1793 until the 1838 Removal. Talking Rock Creek, six miles east, was a settled Cherokee community; Long Swamp and Talona Creeks ran through Cherokee fields. The white oak, ramps, sochan, and ginseng that grow on this kind of mountainside were tended here for thousands of years before the property had a survey line. The Eastern Band's Center for Cherokee Plants still keeps that seedwork going in western North Carolina.
**Notes:** Most sensitive callout — leaned hard on charter restraint. No "we acknowledge," no "we honor," no first-person at all. Just states the historical fact, names the specific creeks (anchors), names the plants the Cherokee actually tended (concrete, not generic), points to the EBCI as a present-day reference without making it sound like a CTA. The phrase "before the property had a survey line" is the closest thing to a posture statement — names the colonial gesture without performing penitence about it. Paul should confirm tone before this ships.

### 2. Tate Mountain Estates
**Heading:** *Tate Mountain Estates*
**Body:** The house stands inside Tate Mountain Estates, the mountain development Col. Sam Tate of the Georgia Marble Company laid out in the late 1920s. Its heart is Lake Sequoyah — thirty-eight acres of dammed creek at about 2,800 feet, completed around 1929 and visible from the fairway when the leaves are off. The lake is roughly a third of a mile down the slope. The name "Tate Tracker" is borrowed from the same Col. Tate, who borrowed it in turn from the mountain.
**Notes:** Leans into the Leopold habit of slow place-naming and the small recursive joke about the name. "Visible from the fairway when the leaves are off" is the field-journal move — a seasonal observation that proves the writer has actually looked at this. The Col. Sam Tate → Tate Mountain → Tate Tracker recursion gives Mom something to smile at without overplaying it.

### 3. Bortle 3 dark sky
**Heading:** *A Bortle 3 sky*
**Body:** The night sky here reads Bortle 3 on the nine-step scale that runs from inner-city orange (9) to the rare unpolluted dark of a desert sky (1). Stephen C. Foster State Park, in the Okefenokee, is Bortle 2 — the only IDA-certified dark-sky site in Georgia — which puts this ridge one tier off the darkest sky the state offers. The Milky Way is visible overhead from May through September on a clear moonless night.
**Notes:** Quiet calibration — the Bortle 3 fact only lands when the reader knows what Bortle 3 is *relative to*. Stephen C. Foster as the in-state reference point does that work. "Reads Bortle 3" is the almanac register; "is Bortle 3" would feel more clinical. Final sentence is a viewing note, not a verdict — describes the sky, doesn't grade the experience.

### 4. Keystone plants for the Blue Ridge
**Heading:** *Keystone plants for the Blue Ridge*
**Body:** A handful of native plant genera do a disproportionate share of the ecological work in this ecoregion. Oaks support more than 400 species of butterfly and moth caterpillars on their leaves; willows around 300; cherries around 200; blueberries and goldenrods carry the herbaceous layer. Those caterpillars are what the warblers and chickadees feed to their nestlings in May and June. The mature oak-hickory forest around the house is doing real work in that food chain.
**Notes:** Pulled straight from NWF Keystone Plants by Ecoregion + Tallamy's *Bringing Nature Home*. Concrete numbers do the heavy lifting (~400, ~300, ~200) — far more credible than "supports lots of biodiversity." Closing sentence anchors generic ecoregion data back to *this* property's actual forest, which is the depth-filter move. Uses "around the house" not "your forest" — third-person, observational.

### 5. Burn ban — Georgia outdoor burning
**Heading:** *Outdoor burning, by season*
**Body:** Georgia's state outdoor-burning ban runs May 1 through September 30 statewide — no permits issued, no open burning of yard debris. The rest of the year, burns require a permit from the Georgia Forestry Commission at gatrees.org. The ban is an air-quality rule, not a fire-danger rule; it covers the months when ground-level ozone pushes hardest.
**Notes:** This is the easiest spot to lapse into productivity-app voice ("DON'T BURN — VIOLATIONS PUNISHABLE BY"). Charter says: describe the rule, don't direct the reader. So: states the dates, states what's required outside the window, ends with the *why* — which is the Leopold move and also genuinely useful (most people don't know it's an air-quality rule). The heading "Outdoor burning, by season" frames it as an almanac entry rather than a warning bar.

### 6. Homegrown National Park
**Heading:** *Homegrown National Park*
**Body:** Doug Tallamy's Homegrown National Park is a grassroots project that asks landowners to register native habitat — any size, any condition — on a single national map. The math is that private land in aggregate dwarfs every public preserve combined, and that biodiversity recovery is mostly a matter of what gets planted at home. The property qualifies on the strength of the existing mature forest alone.
**Notes:** Closest to a CTA the charter allows — Tallamy's project asks for an action (registration), and the field-journal voice has to soften that without erasing it. Solution: describe the project, describe the math behind it, note that the property qualifies — but stop short of "click here to register." If Paul wants to add a quiet link line ("homegrownnationalpark.org") below the paragraph, that's fine; charter would resist any button styled as a CTA. The third sentence ("on the strength of the existing mature forest alone") is the property-specific anchor.
